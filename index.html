Instructions

Lysosomal trapping

Lysosomes are acidic (pH ≈ 5) compared with the cytosol (pH ≈ 7). A neutral amine drug (B:, red) can cross the hydrophobic membrane of the lysosome, but inside it picks up a proton to form the conjugate acid. As a cation, the conjugate acid BH⁺ (blue with a white proton) cannot cross back over the lipid bilayer, and so the drug builds up in the lysosome. This is lysosomal trapping and the degree to which it happens depends on the basicity of the drug.

Henderson–Hasselbalch equation

pH = pKa + log [B:][BH⁺]

According to the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, when the pH of the environment is below the pKa of the drug, most of the molecule exists as the conjugate acid BH⁺. If the pH of the environment is greater than the pKa of the drug, the majority of the molecule will exist in its neutral, basic form B:. The simulation uses this equation to decide, moment to moment, what fraction of the drug is B: or BH⁺ based on the pH of the compartment the molecule is currently located in. Because the lysosome is more acidic, a larger fraction of the drug exists in the ionized state BH⁺ when inside the lysosome.

How to use the simulation

  • Hover over (or tap) the CONTROLS tab at the top to set the drug's pKa, the particle speed, and the number of particles.
  • PLAY starts or pauses the simulation; RESET moves every molecule back into the cytosol.
  • The Lysosomal Trapping Factor compares the drug concentration inside the lysosome with that outside.

Explore

  • Run the simulation at pKa ≈ 5, then ≈ 8. Which drug is trapped more, and why?
  • Then open the TEST YOUR UNDERSTANDING tab on the left to compare two real drug candidates and test your prediction.
